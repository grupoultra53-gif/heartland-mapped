How did one five-mile bridge end twenty-mile traffic jams that split Michigan in half?

Wait... let's rewind.

For decades, reaching Michigan's Upper Peninsula meant lining up for state auto ferries across the Straits of Mackinac.

During summer vacations and deer hunting season, cars backed up for miles—turning a short crossing into an all-day wait.

Worse, brutal winter ice jams could shut the ferries down completely, stranding the Upper Peninsula.

Critics laughed at building a suspension bridge over deep, wind-whipped straits—calling it Mighty Mac's folly.

Then legendary engineer David Steinman stepped up with a brilliant open-grate deck built to let fierce winds pass straight through.

Starting in 1954, thousands of ironworkers battled freezing gales and dizzying heights to spin 42,000 miles of steel cable.

On November 1, 1957, the five-mile Mackinac Bridge finally opened to roaring cheers and honking horns.

Hours of waiting on the dock shrank overnight into a breathtaking ten-minute drive above the Great Lakes.

Today, over two hundred million vehicles have crossed Michigan's beloved Big Mac.
