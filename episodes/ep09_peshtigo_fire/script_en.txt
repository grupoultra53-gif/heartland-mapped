How did America's deadliest wildfire kill five times more people than Chicago on the exact same night—and get forgotten?

Wait... let's rewind.

Everyone knows October 8, 1871, as the night the Great Chicago Fire burned three hundred people's lives away.

Yet 250 miles north in Wisconsin, an even deadlier monster was erupting in the lumber town of Peshtigo.

Months of severe drought had turned a million acres of cut timber and slash piles into bone-dry tinder.

That night, hundred-mile-an-hour winds merged small brush fires into a two-thousand-degree firestorm.

A roaring tornado of flame swallowed Peshtigo in minutes—burning over one million acres across Wisconsin and Michigan.

Terrified families ran for the Peshtigo River, standing neck-deep in freezing water as burning embers rained from the sky.

Between twelve hundred and twenty-five hundred people perished—the deadliest wildfire in recorded history.

Why did the world barely hear about it? The flames melted Peshtigo's telegraph wires in the opening minutes.

While Chicago's tragedy filled every front page, Wisconsin's horror stayed silent for days.
