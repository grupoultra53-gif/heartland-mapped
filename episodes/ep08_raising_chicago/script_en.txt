How did Chicago physically jack up an entire city ten feet out of the mud without closing a single hotel?

Wait... let's rewind.

In the 1850s, Chicago sat on a flat, soggy swamp barely higher than Lake Michigan.

With zero drainage, rainwater and sewage pooled in the streets—triggering a deadly 1854 cholera epidemic.

In 1856, engineer Ellis Chesbrough pitched a wild fix: build sewers above ground, bury them, and lift the whole city up.

Teams dug trenches under massive brick blocks and slid hundreds of iron jackscrews beneath the foundations.

At a whistle blast, hundreds of workers gave every screw a tiny quarter-turn in perfect unison.

In 1861, five hundred men used one thousand jackscrews to lift the six-story Tremont House hotel six feet.

Guests slept in their beds, ate dinner, and strolled out the lobby—never realizing the building was rising.

Wooden shops were even rolled down the streets on logs while shoppers kept browsing inside!

Over a decade, Chicago raised entire city blocks—and saved itself from the swamp forever.
