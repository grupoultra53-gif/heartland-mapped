Why does Michigan own a massive chunk of land physically glued to Wisconsin?

Wait... let's rewind.

In 1835, Michigan and Ohio nearly went to war over a tiny port called the Toledo Strip.

Before an election year, Congress sided with Ohio—blocking Michigan's statehood until it surrendered Toledo.

As a consolation prize, Congress carved nine thousand square miles off Wisconsin Territory—handing Michigan the Upper Peninsula.

Wisconsin wasn't a state yet and couldn't fight back—while Michigan mocked the prize as a frozen swamp.

Then, years later, prospectors struck massive veins of pure copper and mountains of iron ore.

That "worthless" wilderness turned into one of America's richest mineral booms—fueling the entire Industrial Revolution.

Still, for over a century, Michigan's two halves had no road between them—only slow ferries across the Straits of Mackinac.

That isolation forged fierce local pride: "Yoopers" up north, teasing southern Michiganders as "Trolls" living under the bridge.

When the five-mile Mackinac Bridge finally connected them in 1957, Michigan celebrated—and Wisconsin never quite got over it.
