In 1900, Chicago pulled off a jaw-dropping engineering stunt: they forced an entire river to flow backwards... straight toward St. Louis.

Wait—let's rewind.

By the 1880s, booming Chicago dumped its waste into the Chicago River... which flowed straight into Lake Michigan.

The problem? Lake Michigan was Chicago's drinking water. After a massive 1885 storm, deadly disease swept the city.

So engineers hatched a wild plan: dig a 28-mile canal deep enough to reverse gravity and drain the lake south.

For eight years, crews blasted through solid limestone, inventing excavation methods later used on the Panama Canal.

Downriver in Missouri, St. Louis realized Chicago's waste was heading straight for them—and raced to court to stop it.

Chicago didn't wait. Before dawn on January 2nd, 1900, officials quietly snuck out in the freezing dark and blasted open the final dam.

Lake Michigan rushed inland, and for the first time ever, the Chicago River flowed backwards.

Missouri sued Illinois all the way to the Supreme Court... and lost. The justices ruled St. Louis couldn't prove Chicago caused its illness.

Today, the river still flows backwards—hailed as an engineering wonder, and dyed bright green every St. Patrick's Day.

It saved Chicago and fueled a legendary Midwest rivalry. So be honest... pure engineering genius, or the dirtiest trick ever played on St. Louis?
