How did three honey trees nearly start a war between Iowa and Missouri in 1839?

Wait... let's rewind.

In 1816, surveyors drew Missouri's northern border straight east from the Des Moines River rapids.

Twenty years later, Missouri claimed the wrong rapids were used, pushing the border nine miles north into 2,600 square miles of Iowa.

When Missouri sent a tax sheriff, Iowa's governor—the exact same leader from Ohio's Toledo War—had him arrested on the spot.

Then came the breaking point: a Missouri farmer snuck across the line and chopped down three hollow bee trees for their honey.

Furious Iowans fined him a dollar fifty, newspapers dubbed it the Honey War, and both governors mobilized militias.

Over a thousand Iowans marched south in freezing December with rusty muskets, pitchforks, frying pans, and even a sausage stuffer.

Shivering in the bitter cold, both sides realized nobody wanted to die over honey, and marched home without firing a shot.

Ten years later, the U.S. Supreme Court unanimously backed Iowa, marking the 1816 border with permanent iron posts.

Zero casualties, three bee trees lost: America's sweetest standoff.
