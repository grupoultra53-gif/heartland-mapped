How did one sunny Sunday afternoon turn pitch black across six American states in minutes?

Wait... let's rewind.

By the 1930s, decades of deep plowing had stripped away the native prairie grass holding the Great Plains together.

When a punishing multi-year drought hit, millions of acres of rich farmland dried into choking powder.

April 14, 1935, dawned warm and crystal clear—bringing families outside across Kansas, Oklahoma, and Nebraska.

Then on the northern horizon appeared a towering wall of black dirt thousands of feet high, racing south at sixty miles an hour.

In seconds, daylight vanished completely—plunging towns into pitch darkness so thick you couldn't see your own hand.

Static electricity stalled car engines, drifts buried fences, and terrified families stuffed wet sheets under every door.

The very next day, a reporter in Oklahoma coined a name that stuck forever: the Dust Bowl.

Folk legend Woody Guthrie captured that terrifying afternoon in song as thousands packed up for California.

Black Sunday reshaped American farming forever—proving how deeply the Heartland depends on its prairie roots.
