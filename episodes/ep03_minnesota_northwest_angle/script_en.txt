Ever notice that weird little bump on top of Minnesota? You literally have to drive through Canada to reach it... thanks to one terrible map.

Wait—let's rewind.

In 1783, American and British diplomats met in Paris to draw the new border between the U.S. and Canada.

Using a faulty 1755 map, they drew the border straight west from Lake of the Woods to meet the Mississippi River.

When real surveyors hiked north, they hit two snags: the Mississippi started way south... and the lake hid fourteen thousand islands.

In 1818, both sides set the border along the 49th parallel, dropping a straight line south from the lake's northwest corner.

Problem: that corner hid inside a northern bay. Dropping the line south accidentally sliced off 123 square miles... cut off by water.

A chunk of Minnesota was stranded above Canada—reachable only by boat, plane, or a forty-mile drive through another country.

In 1997, angry over Canadian fishing rules, locals even petitioned Congress to leave the U.S. and join Canada before a deal cooled things down.

Today, about a hundred Minnesotans still live there—checking in at a lonely border kiosk, or driving right across the frozen lake in winter.

That old map blunder created the northernmost point of the lower forty-eight—and America's favorite geography quirk.

So be honest... should the U.S. and Canada finally clean up the border, or should Minnesota keep its famous bump forever?
