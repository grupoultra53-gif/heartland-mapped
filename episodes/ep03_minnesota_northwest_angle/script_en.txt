Why does Minnesota have that weird bump sticking into Canada? You drive through another country to reach it—thanks to one bad map.

Wait... let's rewind.

In 1783, U.S. and British diplomats met in Paris to draw the new border.

Using a faulty 1755 map, they drew a straight line west from Lake of the Woods to the Mississippi.

Real surveyors hit two snags: the Mississippi started way south... and the lake hid fourteen thousand islands.

In 1818, they fixed it along the 49th parallel, dropping a line south from the lake's northwest corner.

Problem: that corner hid in a northern bay—accidentally slicing off 123 square miles surrounded by water.

A chunk of Minnesota sat stranded above Canada: reachable only by boat, plane, or a forty-mile border detour.

In 1997, angry over Canadian fishing rules, locals petitioned Congress to join Canada before a deal cooled things down.

Today, a hundred Minnesotans still check in at a lonely border kiosk—or drive straight across the frozen lake in winter.

That map blunder created the northernmost point of the lower forty-eight.
