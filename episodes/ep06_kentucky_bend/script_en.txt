Why is there a chunk of Kentucky completely trapped inside Tennessee and Missouri?

Wait... let's rewind.

In 1779, surveyors drew the straight border line separating Kentucky and Tennessee—stopping short before reaching the Mississippi River.

Then in 1811 and 1812, the massive New Madrid earthquakes violently shook the valley and twisted the Mississippi's path.

When surveyors finally finished the border line, it sliced straight across a giant double horseshoe loop of the river.

That accidental cut left thirty square miles of Kentucky completely detached from the rest of the state.

Tennessee begged Kentucky for decades to hand it over—even offering to trade land elsewhere—but Kentucky flatly refused.

Surrounded on three sides by the rushing Mississippi and Missouri, the only road out leads straight south into Tennessee.

Mark Twain even wrote about its famous frontier feuds when riverboats steamed around the sharp bend.

Today, just a handful of farm families live there—growing soybeans and corn on Kentucky's loneliest island.

To visit their own county courthouse, locals have to drive forty miles through Tennessee first.
