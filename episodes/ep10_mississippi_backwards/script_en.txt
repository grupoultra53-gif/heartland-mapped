How did a Midwestern earthquake make the mighty Mississippi River flow backwards—and ring church bells in Boston?

Wait... let's rewind.

At two in the morning on December 16, 1811, frontier settlers near New Madrid, Missouri, jolted awake to a deafening roar.

Over three terrifying months, three mega-quakes above magnitude seven tore through the heart of North America.

Because the Midwest sits on solid ancient bedrock, shockwaves shook an area ten times wider than San Francisco's 1906 quake.

The tremors rattled President Madison's White House and reportedly rang church bells over a thousand miles away in Boston.

Near the Mississippi, the ground cracked wide open, shooting geysers of sand and water high into the air.

Violent fault shifts buckled the riverbed—creating temporary waterfalls and forcing massive surges of water upstream for hours.

In western Tennessee, an entire forest sank overnight as river water flooded in to create Reelfoot Lake.

Congress even passed the 1815 New Madrid Relief Act—America's very first federal disaster relief law.

And that sleeping New Madrid fault still sits right beneath the Heartland today.
